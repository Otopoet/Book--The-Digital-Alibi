I have sat in the room this book describes more times than I can now count. Not the same room. A different building, a different sector, a different set of faces around the table each time. But the same moment, arriving in the same way. A board is asked, by a regulator, a claimant\textquotesingle s solicitor, or its own internal audit function, what it knew at the moment a specific decision was made. Not what its policy said. Not what its risk framework required. What it knew, at that decision, at that moment. And the board discovers, in real time, in front of the people asking the question, that it cannot answer.

This is not a book about artificial intelligence, in the sense that most books with AI in their title are books about artificial intelligence. It is a book about a much older problem that AI has made acute rather than new. Boards have always been asked to demonstrate the information they held at the moment of a material decision. What has changed is the speed and opacity at which that information is now generated, consumed, and lost. A model produces an output, a human acts on it, and within seconds the precise state of the system that produced that output, what it saw, what it did not see, who reviewed it and on what authority, has begun to decay. Governance frameworks describe intent. They were never built to preserve evidence at the individual decision moment. That gap between intent and evidence is what I have come to call the Digital Alibi, and it is the argument this entire book is built to make precise.

I did not arrive at this argument from a research desk. I arrived at it from watching boards, competent, well-advised, conventionally well-governed boards, fail the same question in the same way, across sectors that have almost nothing else in common. A credit decision. A hiring decision. A procurement award. Different industries, different regulators, identical silence when the forensic question arrived. That pattern, repeated enough times, stops looking like coincidence and starts looking like structure. This book is my attempt to name that structure clearly enough that a board can act on it before the question is asked of them, rather than after.

I want to be direct about what this book is not. It is not a technology primer, and a director does not need to understand how a model is trained to use it. It is not a compliance checklist, though it draws heavily on the regulatory architecture now in force. It is not, in the end, a book about artificial intelligence at all. It is a book about a duty that has existed for as long as boards have existed, the duty to be able to account for what you knew when you decided, applied to a technology that has made discharging that duty far harder and failing to discharge it far more visible.

The chapters that follow draw their evidence from industries that faced a version of this problem before AI made it universal: aviation, financial services, industrial safety, insurance, and medical law among them. Each of those industries built, in response to a reckoning it did not choose the timing of, an architecture that made the previously invisible visible. None of those architectures were obvious before the failure that forced them. All of them look obvious now. That is, I think, the most useful thing a board can take from this book before its own reckoning arrives: the shape of the failure is already known, from other people\textquotesingle s history, and the shape of the response is already available to any board willing to build it in advance.

If it cannot explain itself to a board, it should not be trusted by one. That principle runs through every chapter that follows. It is a simple standard, stated plainly. It is also, as the chapters ahead will show, a standard that almost no organisation currently meets. This book exists to close that distance.
