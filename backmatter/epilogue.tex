\chapter*{Epilogue: The Moment Before the Question Arrives}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Epilogue: The Moment Before the Question Arrives}
\markboth{Epilogue: The Moment Before the Question Arrives}{Epilogue: The Moment Before the Question Arrives}

The room is unremarkable. A conference table. Water glasses. A regulator's representative, courteous and unhurried, with a single sheet of paper in front of him.

You have been here before, at the start of this book. The board across the table had prepared for six weeks. They had briefed their legal counsel, reviewed their governance documentation, and assembled every policy the organisation had produced on artificial intelligence in three years. And when the question came, brief and precise, they could not answer it. Not because wrongdoing had occurred. Because the evidence the question asked for had never been captured, and what was never captured cannot be reconstructed.

This is a different board.

The question, when it comes, is the same. Can you show me the complete information picture available at the moment this AI system produced the output that led to this decision?

The company secretary opens the evidence ledger and retrieves the Alibi Record for the decision named on the regulator's sheet of paper. It takes eleven minutes, most of which is spent confirming the examiner's reference against the Decision Record. The record shows the system, the version, the parameters, and the operational context: Decision Identity. It shows what the system was given, what it retrieved, and, in the Negative Space Record, what it was not given and what it could not see, documented at the output moment, not inferred afterwards: The Information Picture. It shows who reviewed the output, what they were shown, what authority they held to override, and what they decided: The Human Oversight Record. And it shows the named individuals accountable for each governance function at the moment of the decision, from the system owner through the executive sponsor to the board committee that held oversight, assigned at the time and not retrofitted for the meeting: The Accountability Chain.

The examiner reads for some time. Then he asks the second question, the one the first board never reached: how do I know this record was created at the decision moment and not assembled for this examination?

The company secretary shows him the Retrievability Certification: the record is contemporaneous, tamper-evident, and produced from the same ledger, in the same form, as every other governed decision the organisation has made since the architecture went live. This decision was not prepared for. It did not need to be. The record that answers the question was created before anyone knew the question would be asked about this decision rather than any other.

That is the entire difference between the two boards, and it is worth stating exactly, because it is smaller and larger than it appears.

It is smaller than it appears because the second board did not anticipate the question. They did not guess which decision would be examined, which regulator would arrive, or which claimant would issue proceedings. Anticipation is impossible. There were thousands of governed decisions in the ledger, and the examiner chose one. The board's foresight extended no further than the first board's.

It is larger than it appears because the second board did something the first board never did: they stopped treating the question as a hypothetical. They accepted that in a regulated organisation deploying AI in material decisions, the arrival of the question is not a risk to be weighed. It is a scheduled event with an unscheduled date. And once the question is treated as certain, the governance logic inverts. You do not prepare answers. You build the architecture that produces the answer to every question of this kind, for every governed decision, at the moment each decision is made.

Nothing about the second board is exceptional. Their directors are not more diligent than the first board's. Their policies are not better written. Their intentions are not purer. Intentions were never the variable. The first board's governance described intent, and intent is not evidence. The second board's governance produces evidence, and evidence is the only currency the room accepts.

The regulator's question will arrive. The shareholder's challenge will arrive. The litigation will arrive. Not for every board, not this year, perhaps not for several years. But the evidentiary standard is now in place, the instruments that enforce it are now in force, and the professionals who ask the question are now trained to ask it with forensic precision. The moment before the question arrives is the only moment in which anything can be done about the answer.

The board that survives the question will not be the board that had the best governance intentions. It will be the board that built the alibi.
